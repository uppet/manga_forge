# Story Quality Engine

Use this reference when the user asks what makes a good story, how to improve the whole story, whether an idea can become a strong novel, or how a top-tier novelist would shape premise, character, conflict, plot, theme, and reader emotion.

Do not treat "good story" as vague inspiration. Reduce it to dramatic mechanics the author can revise.

## Core Definition

A strong story is a chain of pressured human choices.

At minimum, it needs:

* a reader promise: what emotional experience the story offers
* a protagonist with desire, wound, fear, contradiction, and agency
* an external problem that cannot be ignored
* an antagonist or opposing force with real leverage
* stakes that are personal, social, moral, and practical
* escalating choices where each solution creates a sharper problem
* costs that permanently change power, trust, identity, safety, or knowledge
* cause and effect that makes the plot feel earned
* a theme tested through action, not speeches
* scenes that change something and leave emotional residue
* an ending that feels surprising but inevitable

If the story has only events, lore, vibes, tropes, or cool imagery, it is not yet a strong story.

## The Story Engine Formula

Use this formula to build or diagnose the core:

```text
protagonist wound
+ urgent desire
+ external pressure
+ specific obstacle
+ irreversible choice
+ meaningful cost
+ changed relationship or self-image
+ sharper next pressure
= story momentum
```

If one part is missing, the story usually stalls.

## Top-Tier Novelist Questions

Ask these before praising or drafting:

1. Why this protagonist?
2. Why now?
3. What do they want on the surface?
4. What do they actually need but resist?
5. What lie do they believe about themselves or the world?
6. Who or what has the power to hurt them specifically?
7. What does every victory cost?
8. What information is withheld, misread, or revealed at a price?
9. How does each chapter make the next chapter harder?
10. What final choice proves the story's emotional argument?

If the answer is generic, force it to become specific.

## Reader Promise

Define the promise before expanding the plot.

Examples:

* Wonder: enter a world with rules, beauty, danger, and cost.
* Suspense: solve a question under pressure before the truth destroys someone.
* Romance: watch guarded people risk vulnerability and choose each other under consequence.
* Horror: face vulnerability, dread, bodily threat, uncertainty, and irreversible loss.
* Epic fantasy: watch personal wounds collide with mythic pressure, institutions, and power limits.
* Dark adult drama: watch desire, shame, power, consequence, and moral compromise reshape people.

A story can mix promises, but one promise must dominate. If the promise is unclear, the reader will not know how to care.

## Protagonist Design

Build the protagonist around pressure, not biography.

Minimum profile:

* external goal: what they actively pursue
* internal need: what would make them whole or honest
* wound: what shaped their defensive behavior
* fear: what they would rather avoid than lose
* lie: the false belief driving bad choices
* contradiction: the trait that makes them interesting
* coping mechanism: how they survive pain badly
* moral test: the choice that reveals who they are
* relationship pressure: who exposes their weakness
* voice: how their fear and desire shape language

Avoid protagonists who are only special, chosen, kind, strong, tragic, or mysterious. They need pressure and contradiction.

## Conflict Design

Good conflict is not noise. It is incompatible desire under pressure.

Check:

* Does someone want something now?
* Does someone else, or the world itself, block it?
* Can the conflict be solved by one honest conversation? If yes, it is probably too thin unless avoidance itself has a cost.
* Does the obstacle attack the protagonist's wound or fear?
* Does winning create a new loss?
* Does the antagonist have a coherent goal, method, and leverage?

Escalation should narrow options, not just increase explosions.

## Plot Logic

Plot should feel like consequence, not scheduling.

Use:

```text
because of this -> therefore -> but -> so -> at a cost
```

Avoid:

```text
and then -> and then -> and then
```

Every major turn should come from at least one of these:

* a character choice
* a revealed truth
* a paid cost
* an antagonist move
* a world-rule consequence
* a relationship rupture
* a deadline or resource collapse

Coincidence may start trouble, but it should not solve trouble.

## Scene Engine

Each scene should perform a job.

Minimum scene test:

* viewpoint pressure: what hurts right now?
* objective: what does the character want before the scene ends?
* obstacle: what resists the objective?
* tactic shift: what does the character try when the first tactic fails?
* turn: what changes the scene's direction?
* choice: what decision reveals character?
* cost: what becomes worse because of the choice?
* residue: what feeling or image remains?
* hook: what question or pressure pulls forward?

If a scene only explains, travels, introduces, or decorates, compress it or rebuild it around pressure.

## Theme Through Action

Theme is not a message pasted on top. It is the pressure pattern of the whole book.

Strong theme appears when:

* the protagonist's lie is tested repeatedly
* side characters embody different answers to the same question
* the world rewards the wrong value
* the climax forces a value choice
* the ending shows the cost of change or refusal to change

Do not make characters explain the theme unless the scene gives them a believable reason and the line creates pressure.

## Information and Mystery

Information should create hunger.

Useful sequence:

```text
question -> clue -> misread -> consequence -> sharper question -> costly reveal
```

Bad sequence:

```text
lore explanation -> correct answer -> no consequence
```

Reveal truth in pieces. A good reveal does not end curiosity; it changes what the reader fears.

## Emotional Impact

Emotion lands when it is specific, embodied, and consequential.

Prefer:

* behavior over labels
* silence over speeches
* sensory detail over abstract feeling
* contradiction over clean emotion
* aftermath over immediate explanation

After major events, slow down enough for residue: guilt, shame, grief, desire, distrust, relief, disgust, or changed self-image.

## Ending Quality

A strong ending is earned, not merely surprising.

Check:

* Does the climax force the protagonist's central moral or emotional choice?
* Does the final choice grow from earlier wounds, failures, and costs?
* Does the resolution answer the reader promise?
* Are the main consequences visible?
* Does the ending leave emotional residue, not just plot cleanup?
* If it sets up a sequel, does it still complete this book's core arc?

## Common Failure Modes

Flag these directly:

* Cool setting, weak human engine.
* Passive protagonist moved by prophecy, coincidence, or other characters.
* Strong opening, sagging middle with repeated beats.
* Antagonist has style but no leverage.
* Stakes are big but not personal.
* Characters choose convenience instead of desire or fear.
* World rules solve problems without cost.
* Romance, friendship, or betrayal happens without pressure history.
* Mystery reveals too much too early or hides too much unfairly.
* Ending depends on a new power, clue, or rule introduced too late.
* Prose is polished but the dramatic engine is weak.

## Story Quality Audit Format

When diagnosing a whole story, use this structure:

```text
Story Quality Verdict:
One direct paragraph on whether the story has a working dramatic engine.

Core Promise:
What the reader is being promised, and whether the draft fulfills it.

Main Engine:
Protagonist desire + wound + conflict + stakes + antagonist pressure.

Biggest Strength:
Name the strongest real asset, not generic praise.

Top 5 Structural Problems:
1. Problem
   Why it hurts
   Fix

Missing Pressure:
Where the story lacks cost, consequence, urgency, opposition, or emotional risk.

Best Next Revision:
The one change that would improve the whole story fastest.

Optional Rewrite Target:
A stronger premise, chapter spine, character arc, or scene rebuild.
```

## Good Story Blueprint Format

When helping build a new story, use this structure:

```text
Reader Promise:

One-Sentence Premise:

Protagonist:
- external goal
- wound
- fear
- lie
- contradiction
- moral test

Opposing Force:
- goal
- leverage
- method
- personal connection to protagonist pressure

Story Spine:
1. Opening pressure
2. First irreversible choice
3. Promise of the premise
4. Midpoint reversal
5. Cost of false victory
6. Low point
7. Final moral choice
8. Ending residue

Chapter Engine:
How each chapter creates goal, obstacle, reveal, cost, and hook.

Theme in Action:
The value conflict tested by choices.
```

## Revision Priority

Fix in this order:

1. Reader promise and protagonist engine.
2. Core conflict and antagonist leverage.
3. Plot cause and effect.
4. Stakes, costs, and consequences.
5. Chapter and scene function.
6. Continuity and world-rule consistency.
7. Dialogue and prose style.
8. Publishing package or surface polish.

Do not line-edit a story whose engine is broken unless the user explicitly asks for a style sample.
